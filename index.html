01 / Ideas

What I keep coming back to.

Referral habits, benefit design, supportive care, and clinical workflows decide who gets what. Four positions that run through the research, the clinic, and the advisory work, each with data behind it.

Most of what decides trial enrollment is structural.

Fewer than one in twenty patients in community oncology enroll in a therapeutic trial. In our national analysis of more than 200,000 patients, physician- and practice-level factors explained more of the variation in enrollment than patient characteristics did, and their share has grown over the past decade. Willingness is not the constraint. Infrastructure is.

See the enrollment variation work

The referral is the intervention.

A fifth of physicians generate four fifths of enrollments, and close to a third of physicians in trial-active practices enroll no one. A trial opportunity becomes real when someone recognizes it and connects the patient to it. Screening support, navigation, and workflow design around the referring oncologist move that number.

Explore the trial access program

Benefit design is a clinical variable.

Which plan a patient holds shapes which oncologists are in reach, which supportive medications need prior authorization, and how quickly treatment starts. Insurance belongs in the same conversation as stage and biomarker status, and it can be measured with the same rigor.

Read the treatment initiation study

Clinical AI needs a workflow test, not a benchmark.

Nearly every published use of large language models in oncology trials has been evaluated retrospectively on curated data. What matters is what happens after the tool produces an answer: who reviews it, what action follows, and whether patients end up with decisions that reflect their own priorities.

Explore the AI and communication work

Implementation program · Ongoing

Making trial referral part of routine care.

Principal Investigator, American Cancer Society IMPACT award (2025–2027)
Director, GU Clinical Trial Optimization, Parkland Cancer Center
Parkland Health & UTSW regional sites

The problem. An open prostate cancer trial does not, by itself, create a reliable path from the clinic visit to screening and enrollment. The missing step differs by site: at a community site it is often transportation or trust, at a safety-net hospital it is usually a coordinator, a reflex order, or physician time.

My work. I lead a program that maps barriers site by site, then removes the specific one: physician engagement and a redesigned referral workflow, dedicated GU coordinator support, navigation and travel funds, and patient-facing plain-language trial summaries. Under the Robert A. Winn Excellence in Clinical Trials award, the same approach expanded enrollment of Hispanic/Latino and Black patients in HARMONY, a phase 2 trial of niraparib plus abiraterone in HRR-altered metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

Where it stands. Navigation and physician-engagement programs doubled enrollment of underrepresented patients. Referral tools are in use; enrollment among underrepresented patients is the primary outcome as implementation continues.

Which step is missing at this site, and who needs to own it?

Understanding why trial enrollment varies.

Principal Investigator, Conquer Cancer / ASCO Career Development Award
National EHR-derived analysis (Flatiron Health, 2010–2024)
Findings presented at the 2025 ASCO Quality Care Symposium

The question. How much does trial participation depend on the physician and practice a patient happens to see?

My work. A cohort of 205,743 patients with advanced solid tumors treated by 1,664 physicians across 107 trial-active community practices. Mixed-effects models decompose enrollment variation into patient, physician, and practice components; companion interviews with oncologists use the COM-B behavioral framework to explain the differences.

What we are learning. Enrollment was 5.3%. Physician- and practice-level factors together explained more variation than patient characteristics, and the practice share has roughly doubled since 2010. Twenty percent of physicians accounted for eighty percent of enrollments; nearly a third of physicians in trial-active practices enrolled no one.

The next question: which differences in referral behavior and practice support can be changed by an intervention?

Workflow & AI · Ongoing

Closing the biomarker test-to-treatment loop.

Principal Investigator, NCCN / Pfizer Quality Improvement Award
UTSW Simmons Cancer Center & Parkland Health
Member, Simmons AI Steering Committee

The problem. Guideline-recommended germline and somatic testing in metastatic prostate cancer is ordered inconsistently, results sit in unstructured reports, and actionable findings do not reliably turn into targeted therapy, least of all in safety-net settings.

My work. An Epic-embedded reflex pathway that orders paired germline and somatic sequencing at diagnosis, escalates to ctDNA when tissue fails, and surfaces every biomarker-positive patient on a molecular-actionability dashboard tied to a multidisciplinary huddle and molecular tumor board. Black and Hispanic patients are pre-specified as the index population for every process and outcome metric.

Also here. Plain-language trial summaries co-designed with patients and community partners, including LLM-generated drafts, now used at the point of referral. Patients remember visits, rides, placebo, and cost; the summaries answer those first.

What would it take for this tool to work in this clinic, and how would we know?

Supportive care & access · MASCC

Treating supportive care as an access problem.

Co-Chair, Health Access and Disparities Committee, MASCC (2025–; member since 2023)
Co-Investigator, NCI R37 UNCOVER-PC

The question. Who gets palliative care, symptom management, and the practical support that keeps people in treatment, and what stands in the way?

My work. Transportation barriers were the starting point: cancer survivors who delay care for lack of a ride have roughly twice the mortality risk (JAMA Oncology, JNCI). From there, financial hardship and social needs, the value of early palliative care, prior authorization and cost barriers for supportive medications, and MASCC's international surveys of financial toxicity and disparities in supportive care.

Supportive care fails quietly, one missed ride or denied prescription at a time. Measuring it is the first step to funding it.

Making insurance visible in cancer care.

Medicare Advantage & Part D
Benefit design, networks & treatment access
SEER-Medicare, CMS plan benefit and formulary files, Flatiron Health

The question. How does the insurance a patient holds relate to the cancer care they can reach?

My work. Linking plan benefit packages, formularies, claims, and real-world clinical cohorts to study zero-premium plan growth, oncologist networks, primary care cost sharing, supportive and generic oncology drug access, and time to treatment under Medicare Advantage versus traditional Medicare.

03 / In the pipeline

Under review and under way.

Findings from manuscripts under review are summarized at the level already presented at national meetings.

Variation in clinical trial enrollment among physicians and practices in community oncology

Under review

The national Flatiron Health analysis described above: 205,743 patients, 1,664 physicians, 107 practices; physician and practice factors outweigh patient factors; 20% of physicians account for 80% of enrollments.

Racial and ethnic disparities in trial enrollment across community practices

Under review

Companion analysis asking whether enrollment gaps for Black and Hispanic patients persist after accounting for the physician and practice they see, and how much of the gap is explained by where care happens.

Large language models across the oncology clinical trial lifecycle: a scoping review

Under review

PRISMA-ScR review from eligibility screening to data extraction. Of 2,546 records, 17 studies qualified, all retrospective or benchmark-based; none evaluated a tool prospectively inside a live trial workflow.

Two commentaries on clinical AI: value alignment, and licensing autonomous systems

Under review

One argues that guideline-aligned decision support risks flattening patient values in preference-sensitive oncology decisions; the other that a licensure model for autonomous clinical AI needs a change taxonomy and regression testing.

Physician behavior and trial referral: COM-B interviews

Ongoing · ASCO CDA

Interviews with community and academic oncologists using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation framework to explain why some physicians refer and others do not, feeding the design of referral-step interventions.

Patient preferences for cancer care access and trial participation

Ongoing · DCE

Discrete choice experiments quantifying how patients trade off travel, visit burden, cost, placebo risk, and expected benefit, to inform how trials are offered and explained.

05 / About

The clinic shapes
the questions.

I see patients at UT Southwestern's Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center and at Parkland Health, Dallas County's safety-net system. That split week is central to how I think about care.

Two patients can have the same tumor, biomarker, and treatment recommendation, yet face very different paths because of insurance, referral patterns, transportation, or access to testing.

I turn those clinical observations into research questions and practical work on care delivery. In clinic, I care for people with prostate, bladder, kidney, and testicular cancers and serve as site investigator on industry and investigator-initiated trials, including a global phase 3 registration study of a STEAP1×CD3 bispecific in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. In research, I follow questions about trial access, supportive care, payer policy, and the oncology workflow across cancer types.

I trained in medicine in China, studied epidemiology at Yale, and completed residency at Mount Sinai and hematology/oncology fellowship at Roswell Park before joining UT Southwestern in 2023.

My path as an international medical graduate also informs my commitment to mentorship. I co-founded the ANACP Mentoring Program, now 60+ mentors and 100+ mentees, and serve in ASCO's IMG Community of Practice.
