01 / Focus

Four lines of work, one question

Who actually gets high-quality cancer care, and what in the system decides it? Each line below attacks that question from a different angle: the trial referral, the supportive care gap, the insurance plan, and the algorithm.

Clinical trial accessIntervention

Getting underrepresented patients onto prostate cancer trials

I lead UT Southwestern's prostate cancer clinical trial access program across a county safety-net hospital (Parkland Health) and community regional sites, supported by the American Cancer Society. The work targets the referral step, where most enrollment is lost, and pairs it with national real-world analyses of why some physicians and practices enroll and others do not.

Insurance design & deliveryHealth services research

How benefit design shapes cancer care delivery

Medicare Advantage now covers most beneficiaries, and zero-premium plans dominate that market. I link plan benefit files, formularies, claims, and registry data to measure how plan design relates to networks, prior authorization, supportive medication access, and time to treatment for patients with cancer.

  • Zero-premium Medicare Advantage: growth concentrated in counties with high cancer mortality (JNCI 2026); trends in socioeconomically vulnerable areas (Health Affairs Scholar 2025); access to generic abiraterone and imatinib (Health Affairs Scholar 2026).
  • Plan design and cancer care: primary care cost sharing in MA (JAMA Health Forum 2026); oncologist networks under MA plans (JAMA Network Open 2026); delayed novel hormonal therapy for metastatic prostate cancer in MA versus traditional Medicare (JCO Oncology Practice 2026).
  • Supplemental benefits and utilization management: non-emergency medical transportation benefits, GLP-1 coverage in Part D, and vertical integration of oncology practices.
  • Medicaid expansion: racial disparity in timely multidisciplinary treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (JNCI 2023).
  • Data: SEER-Medicare, CMS Plan Benefit Package and Part D formulary files, Flatiron Health, national surveys (NHIS, HINTS, MEPS).

02 / In the pipeline

Work under review and under way

Where the clinical trial access and AI work stands right now. Findings from manuscripts under review are summarized at the level already presented at national meetings.

Variation in clinical trial enrollment among physicians and practices in community oncology

Under review

National EHR-derived cohort (Flatiron Health, 2010–2024): 205,743 patients with advanced solid tumors, 1,664 physicians, 107 trial-active practices. Enrollment 5.3%. Physician- and practice-level factors explain more variation than patient factors; 20% of physicians account for 80% of enrollments. Core deliverable of the ASCO Career Development Award; abstracts presented at the 2025 ASCO Quality Care Symposium.

Racial and ethnic disparities in trial enrollment across community practices

Under review

Companion analysis asking whether enrollment gaps for Black and Hispanic patients persist after accounting for the physician and practice they see, and how much of the gap is explained by where care happens rather than who the patient is.

Large language models across the oncology clinical trial lifecycle: a scoping review

Under review

PRISMA-ScR review of peer-reviewed LLM applications from eligibility screening to data extraction. Of 2,546 records, 17 studies qualified, all retrospective or benchmark-based; none evaluated a tool prospectively inside a live trial workflow. Sets priorities for prospective, equity-focused evaluation.

Two commentaries on clinical AI: value alignment, and licensing autonomous systems

Under review

One argues that the larger risk of guideline-aligned LLM decision support in oncology is value flattening for preference-sensitive decisions; the other that a licensure model for autonomous clinical AI needs a change taxonomy and regression testing, because the licensed object keeps changing.

Prostate cancer trial access program at Parkland Health and UTSW regional sites

Ongoing · ACS IMPACT

Site-specific barrier removal: navigation and travel support at community sites, a dedicated GU coordinator and physician workflow redesign at the safety-net hospital. Physician-facing referral tools and patient-facing plain-language trial summaries are deployed; enrollment of underrepresented patients is the primary outcome.

Closing the biomarker test-to-treatment loop in metastatic prostate cancer

Ongoing · NCCN

Epic-embedded reflex germline and somatic sequencing, ctDNA salvage when tissue fails, and a molecular-actionability dashboard tied to a multidisciplinary huddle, with Black and Hispanic patients pre-specified as the index population for every process and outcome metric.

Physician behavior and trial referral: COM-B interviews

Ongoing · ASCO CDA

Qualitative interviews with community and academic oncologists using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation model to explain why some physicians refer and others do not, feeding the design of referral-step interventions.

Patient preferences for cancer care access and trial participation

Ongoing · DCE

Discrete choice experiments quantifying how patients trade off travel, visit burden, cost, placebo risk, and expected benefit when deciding about treatment and trial participation, to inform how trials are offered and explained.

03 / Perspectives

How I think about it

Six positions that run through the research, the clinic, and the advisory work. Each has data behind it, most of it from national real-world cohorts and from running a trial access program at a county hospital.

Most of what decides trial enrollment is structural.

Fewer than one in twenty patients in community oncology enroll in a therapeutic trial. In our national analysis of more than 200,000 patients, physician- and practice-level factors explained more of the variation in enrollment than patient characteristics did, and their share has grown over the past decade. Willingness is not the constraint. Infrastructure is.

The referral is the intervention.

A fifth of physicians generate four fifths of enrollments, and close to a third of physicians in trial-active practices enroll no one. Screening support, navigation, and workflow design around the referring oncologist move that number. Awareness campaigns alone do not.

In the safety net, access is a workflow problem before it is a trust problem.

At a county hospital the missing step is usually a coordinator, a reflex test order, an interpreter, or a ride, not a patient's refusal. Mapping barriers site by site, then removing the specific one, has done more for enrollment of Black and Hispanic patients in our program than any general diversity plan.

Patients remember logistics, not mechanisms.

What patients retain from a trial conversation is how many visits, how many rides, whether there is a placebo, and who pays. A one-page summary that answers those questions does more for informed consent than a thirty-page document, and it is the tool we now hand out at the point of referral.

Benefit design is a clinical variable.

Which plan a patient holds shapes which oncologists are in reach, which supportive medications need prior authorization, and how quickly treatment starts. It belongs in the same conversation as stage and biomarker status, and it can be measured with the same rigor.

AI earns its place inside the workflow, not on a benchmark.

Nearly every published use of large language models in oncology trials has been evaluated retrospectively on curated data. The tools worth adopting are the ones tested in a live clinic, with enrollment, time to treatment, and equity as the endpoints, and with the patient's own priorities kept in the loop.

09 / Background

Where this comes from

I trained in medicine in China and in epidemiology at Yale, then completed internal medicine residency at Mount Sinai and hematology/oncology fellowship at Roswell Park. I joined UT Southwestern in 2023 with a joint appointment in the O'Donnell School of Public Health, and I see patients at both the Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center and Parkland Health, the Dallas County safety-net system.

That split week is the whole method. A patient at Parkland and a patient at Simmons can have the same tumor, the same biomarker, and the same guideline, and end up on different treatment because of a benefit design, a referral habit, or a missing ride. My research exists to make those differences visible and measurable, and my implementation work exists to remove them one workflow at a time.

As an international medical graduate, I also co-founded the ANACP Mentoring Program and serve on ASCO's IMG Community of Practice, because the workforce that staffs many of the country's underserved oncology practices deserves the same structural attention as the patients.
