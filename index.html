01 / Focus

Four lines of work, one question

Who actually gets high-quality cancer care, and what in the system decides it? Each line below attacks that question from a different angle: the trial referral, the supportive care gap, the insurance plan, and the algorithm.

Insurance design & deliveryHealth services research

What a zero-premium plan costs a patient with cancer

Medicare Advantage now covers most beneficiaries, and zero-premium plans dominate that market. I link plan benefit files, formularies, claims, and registry data to measure what the front-end savings trade away at the back end: networks, prior authorization, and time to treatment.

  • Zero-premium Medicare Advantage: growth concentrated in counties with high cancer mortality (JNCI 2026); trends in socioeconomically vulnerable areas (Health Affairs Scholar 2025); access to generic abiraterone and imatinib (Health Affairs Scholar 2026).
  • Plan design and cancer care: primary care cost sharing in MA (JAMA Health Forum 2026); oncologist networks under MA plans (JAMA Network Open 2026); delayed novel hormonal therapy for metastatic prostate cancer in MA versus traditional Medicare (JCO Oncology Practice 2026).
  • Supplemental benefits and utilization management: non-emergency medical transportation benefits, GLP-1 coverage in Part D, and vertical integration of oncology practices.
  • Medicaid expansion: racial disparity in timely multidisciplinary treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer (JNCI 2023).
  • Data: SEER-Medicare, CMS Plan Benefit Package and Part D formulary files, Flatiron Health, national surveys (NHIS, HINTS, MEPS).
Read: Zero-premium MA growth in high cancer-mortality counties · JNCI 2026 · Primary care cost sharing in MA · JAMA Health Forum 2026

02 / Perspectives

How I think about it

Five positions that run through the research, the clinic, and the advisory work. Each is falsifiable, and each has data behind it.

Free is expensive.

A zero-premium plan does not remove cost; it moves cost from the premium to access. The plans grow fastest where cancer mortality is highest, and the patients who choose them on price are the ones least able to absorb a narrow network or a prior-authorization delay.

Insurance design has toxicities. Monitor them like drug toxicities.

Prior authorization, network breadth, formulary tiers, and quantity limits are active ingredients with dose-response effects on time to treatment and survival. We grade chemotherapy toxicity in every note. We should be able to grade plan toxicity at the county level.

The trial enrollment bottleneck is the referral, not the patient.

When a trial is actually offered, most patients, including Black and Hispanic patients, will consider it. Enrollment concentrates in a small minority of physicians. Fix the workflow around the referring oncologist before spending another dollar on awareness campaigns.

Patients remember logistics, not mechanisms.

In our interviews, what patients retain from a trial conversation is how many visits, how many rides, whether there is a placebo, and who pays. A one-page summary that answers those questions does more for informed consent than a thirty-page document.

The larger risk of clinical AI is value flattening, not hallucination.

Models tuned to majority preference treat a patient who prioritizes his hands over three months of median survival as an error to correct. AI in oncology should be evaluated inside real workflows, with preference-concordant care and equity as endpoints, and governed with a change taxonomy rather than a one-time exam.

07 / Background

Where this comes from

I trained in medicine in China and in epidemiology at Yale, then completed internal medicine residency at Mount Sinai and hematology/oncology fellowship at Roswell Park. I joined UT Southwestern in 2023 with a joint appointment in the O'Donnell School of Public Health, and I see patients at both the Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center and Parkland Health, the Dallas County safety-net system.

That split week is the whole method. A patient at Parkland and a patient at Simmons can have the same tumor, the same biomarker, and the same guideline, and end up on different treatment because of a benefit design, a referral habit, or a missing ride. My research exists to make those differences visible and measurable, and my implementation work exists to remove them one workflow at a time.

As an international medical graduate, I also co-founded the ANACP Mentoring Program and serve on ASCO's IMG Community of Practice, because the workforce that staffs many of the country's underserved oncology practices deserves the same structural attention as the patients.
